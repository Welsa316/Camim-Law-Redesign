What Is TPS Within the U.S. Immigration System?

Temporary Protected Status (TPS), as the name suggests, is an immigration status for non-citizens in the United States that is not permanent.

Congress created TPS in 1990, when it reformed the Immigration and Nationality Act, which makes it a relatively recent immigration benefit.

TPS is for citizens of countries that the U.S. Congress or the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) has designated.

Its aim is to protect people from designated countries who might face dangerous conditions or significant hardship if they went back home.

The United States designates countries for TPS when a humanitarian crisis arises from natural disasters, armed conflict, civil unrest, generalized violence, or other extraordinary and temporary conditions.

Typically, a country is designated for TPS after the government concludes that, given its current situation, it is not safe to return there.

Put simply, the U.S. Government, through Congress or the Executive Branch, can designate countries for Temporary Protected Status (TPS) and give their citizens a temporary immigration status allowing them to stay legally in the United States for as long as the conditions behind the designation continue.

Who Is Eligible for TPS?

In general, a person applying for Temporary Protected Status (TPS) must satisfy three basic requirements.

The first is to show that they are a citizen of the designated country, or a stateless person who habitually resided there.

The easiest way to prove citizenship is a passport or birth certificate issued by that country’s authorities.

The second is to show physical presence in the United States on the date the TPS designation became official.

The Department of Homeland Security (DHS) usually publishes a notice in the Federal Register stating that citizens of the designated country who were physically present in the U.S. before a certain date are eligible for the temporary status.

That date usually falls before the designation is officially published. Physical presence before the set date can be shown in several ways, for example with bank statements, leases, pay stubs, insurance receipts, or school records.

Any document or sworn statement that confirms a person was in the U.S. can be submitted to immigration authorities.

Finally, the applicant must show continuous residence in the United States from the time of designation until the application is formally filed. Even so, brief or accidental absences from U.S. territory may be accepted in exceptional circumstances.

Travel records from U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP), copies of passport pages, or other proof of the physical presence described above can be used to show that the applicant has stayed in the U.S. since the TPS designation.

What is more, a TPS applicant must not be inadmissible under immigration law.

Inadmissibility is a concept from the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), which, under specific circumstances, keeps certain people from entering or remaining in the United States.

Under the INA, inadmissibility covers people who have certain medical conditions, who have committed certain kinds of crimes, who have violated immigration laws, or who pose a risk to U.S. security.

In this context, that means the applicant must not have committed a felony or two misdemeanors, since most grounds of inadmissibility are waived for TPS applicants, particularly those tied to violations of immigration law.

What Does TPS Provide Once the Application Is Approved?

TPS has two principal legal effects: holders are protected from deportation, and they are authorized to work legally in the United States.

TPS applicants do not, however, have to request a work permit right away. A TPS holder can also ask for authorization to travel abroad.

Protection from deportation can mean several things: that someone who entered the U.S. unlawfully will not be deported; that someone who came in on a temporary visa and fell out of status will not be placed in removal proceedings; that someone refused a visa or other relief will not be removed; or that someone already in active removal proceedings can have those proceedings suspended.

In short, as long as TPS remains valid, the holder cannot be removed from the United States.

Employment Authorization with TPS

When applying for TPS, a person can request a work permit at the same time.

If they prefer not to do so then, they can request it once the TPS application has been approved.

Under the TPS category (A-12 / C-19), a work permit lets the holder work legally in the United States without significant restrictions.

These permits are issued for the same period as the TPS status itself and can be renewed as long as the TPS designation stays active.

Does TPS Lead to Permanent Residence?

At the time of writing, the Immigration and Nationality Act does not allow TPS holders to adjust their immigration status to that of a lawful permanent resident solely because they have TPS.

Even so, obtaining an immigrant visa while keeping TPS may make it possible for a TPS holder to become a permanent resident.

The main purpose of TPS is to give nationals of a designated country temporary protection, so that they do not have to return home and face danger.

When someone holds two nationalities, however, it is possible that they could seek refuge or residence in a country other than the TPS-designated one.

That does not stop a dual citizen from applying for TPS, but it does call for a close look at the applicant’s actual ties to the designated or non-designated country.

To decide whether the applicant has settled permanently in that third country, factors such as how the second citizenship was obtained, language barriers, time spent living there, how often they travel, family ties, property, or other connections to that country may be weighed.

If that is the case, the TPS application is unlikely to be approved, since the person is considered able to find safe refuge elsewhere.

What Happens If My TPS Is Canceled?

If your TPS is canceled or about to be, it is important to consult an immigration attorney to explore other options based on family, employment, or humanitarian grounds.
